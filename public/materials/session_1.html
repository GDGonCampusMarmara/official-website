01

Why architecture feels difficult today

Students are not short on information. The harder part is learning how to choose, compare, and adapt ideas when the conditions keep changing.

Starting point

Architecture starts mattering the moment software outlives the first version that made it work.

Requirements change. Teams change. Traffic changes. Data shape changes. The cost of early design decisions usually appears later, when change arrives faster than the system can absorb it. That is why architecture stays tied to delivery across time.

Useful framing
  • Architecture defines boundaries and responsibilities.
  • Architecture affects how fast a team can change a system.
  • Architecture decides what becomes easy and what becomes expensive.
  • Architecture is connected to delivery, migration, and operations.

04

Current tendencies shaping system architecture

These reflect recurring pressures that real systems and teams face today.

Important
Distributed computing is a normal baseline

Multiple services, queues, async jobs, external APIs, and partial failure are common. Latency, retries, idempotency, and observability are standard concerns.

Important
Cloud-native thinking is mostly about operating constraints

Elasticity, failure handling, managed services, and operational visibility shape design choices. Cloud changes how software is structured.

Important
Platform engineering tries to reduce delivery friction

Teams build internal platforms, templates, and paved paths so development becomes more repeatable and less dependent on tribal knowledge.

Important
AI-aware architecture adds new system concerns

Probabilistic outputs, tool usage, evaluation, permission boundaries, memory, and observability change how systems are tested and operated.

Context
Modernization is continuous, not a one-time rewrite

Code, data, and architecture evolve while the system keeps running. Migration patterns and compatibility strategies become part of architecture.

Context-sensitive
Specialized patterns must answer a real pressure

CQRS, event sourcing, data mesh, actor systems, plugin architectures, and FaaS can be strong fits when their complexity is worth the cost.

05

Paradigms worth exploring in this first session

Each pattern below shows a different answer to a different pressure. The diagrams are intentionally simple, just enough to make the shape visible during the talk.

Patterns that shape how teams work

These are less about clever code and more about deciding who owns what, how fast change can move, and how teams stay autonomous without chaos.

Vertical slicing

Deliver value through end-to-end slices

Instead of organizing the project around technical layers first, vertical slicing cuts through UI, application logic, and data for one feature at a time. This supports early value delivery and easier adaptation when requirements move.

  • feature-focused structure
  • works well when priorities change often
  • keeps business capability closer to code boundaries
Self-Contained Systems

Package a business capability with its own UI, logic, and data

Self-Contained Systems aim for stronger autonomy than a shared layered application. Each system owns a business area end to end, which improves team independence and keeps change boundaries clearer.

  • end-to-end ownership
  • team autonomy
  • clear business boundaries
Data mesh

Domain ownership sounds great until the governance bill arrives

Data mesh is usually pitched as liberation from the central data team. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it just distributes confusion. It only works when teams can actually own data quality, contracts, and operational responsibility.

  • domain ownership
  • data as a product
  • federated governance

Patterns that shape how data flows

These patterns matter when reads, writes, history, and runtime workload start pulling in different directions.

Event sourcing

Great for audit trails. Dangerous when used as a personality trait.

Event sourcing can be excellent when the history itself matters: auditability, replay, temporal debugging, and systems where the business cares about how state changed. Outside that, it often buys more ceremony than value.

  • append-only event history
  • strong fit for traceability and replay
  • costly when the domain does not need history as a first-class concept
CQRS

Most teams adopt CQRS for the wrong reasons

It earns its complexity in roughly one system in ten. Read and write models split cleanly when the workloads, scaling profiles, or business rules are genuinely different. Knowing the other nine is the architectural skill.

  • separates reads from writes
  • useful when read and write pressures diverge sharply
  • not a free upgrade for ordinary CRUD systems
FaaS

Useful tool. Terrible religion.

Functions as a Service shine when triggered work is small, event-shaped, and operationally spiky. The mistake is treating them as the default unit of architecture instead of one execution model among many.

  • small focused execution units
  • good fit for event handling and automation
  • works best as part of a larger design, not the whole philosophy

Patterns that shape how systems coordinate

Once the system crosses process boundaries, coordination becomes the real tax collector: latency, retries, ordering, visibility, and failure.

Distributed computing

Design for latency, failure, and coordination

As soon as a system spans multiple nodes or services, assumptions change. Retries, ordering, duplication, and consistency trade-offs become normal design concerns.

  • partial failure is expected
  • communication has cost
  • coordination and observability become central
Actor-based model

Model behavior as isolated actors that communicate by messages

Actor-based systems can be a useful alternative to object-centric thinking when concurrency, isolation, and message flow matter more than shared mutable state.

  • isolation by default
  • message-driven behavior
  • good fit for concurrency-heavy systems
Event-driven agent systems

Communication through events with looser connections

For agent systems, event-driven communication can be useful when you want extension, monitoring, and evolution to stay manageable. New listeners can join without every participant needing to know every other participant.

  • looser coupling between participants
  • easier to extend with new consumers
  • better fit when workflows need observability

Patterns that shape how systems evolve

Most architecture work is not greenfield design. It is deciding how change enters an already running system without wrecking the business.

Strangler Fig Pattern

Modernize while the existing system keeps running

New behavior grows around the legacy system and traffic is gradually moved. Functional expectations stay stable for users while the system changes underneath.

  • incremental migration
  • legacy remains live during transition
  • modernization becomes a flow, not a big-bang event
Emerging architecture

Design enough to move, then refine through feedback

Emerging architecture is useful when requirements are still settling. It avoids pretending that everything can be known upfront and treats architecture as something clarified through delivery and iteration.

  • iterative design
  • feedback-driven
  • adaptable to learning and change
Microkernel / Plugin architecture

Keep a stable core and extend around it

This approach works well when a system needs many extensions, custom rules, or product variations. The core stays focused while plugins add specialized behavior.

  • stable core
  • extensibility
  • good for long-lived products
06

Learning architecture by building

The most useful architectural skill is recognizing a missing layer before the industry has named it. These three movements show how that skill is built — through past projects, through the gaps still open, and through what current work is teaching.

Movement 1 · Early

Building before the ecosystem catches up

At the time, the work looks too early, too niche, or slightly out of phase with the market. Then a few years later, the language changes, the tooling improves, the surrounding constraints soften, and suddenly the same ideas no longer seem eccentric. They seem inevitable.

In infrastructure, the real engineering pressure shows up at the edges first — execution safety, tenancy boundaries, runtime assumptions, the uncomfortable details polished abstractions prefer not to mention.

Isolation, execution environments, and operational boundaries — before those concerns became central in mainstream container conversations.

Movement 2 · Missing

Naming the layers the ecosystem still has not built

You can see the shape of a missing layer before the industry has named it. That is the frustrating part of infrastructure work — and also the part that makes the next decade of architecture interesting. The skill is seeing the gap clearly and naming it early.

Once systems become more distributed and more delegated, communication patterns shape the architecture itself. A weak communication layer quietly makes everything above it harder to reason about and operate.

Open Serverless

A dependable, engineering-first, EU-based serverless foundation. Not just a regional wish — a question of trust, operational quality, regulatory context, and infrastructure that feels durable rather than opportunistic.

A serious multi-host agent architecture, built against the assumption that agents live in one process, one host, one loop. Once agents become operationally meaningful, the architecture drifts toward multiple hosts, multiple contexts, multiple execution surfaces.

Movement 3 · Concrete

Building the system that teaches you what trade-offs really cost

The important part of any new technology is usually where the abstractions are still too soft, and where the real engineering pressure will eventually show up. The only way to find those places is to build something real and let it push back.

Building real systems is the fastest way to understand architecture, because trade-offs stop being theoretical.

Docmate

Document RAG on a runtime OLAP database — DuckDB plus vector similarity search. The architectural lesson goes beyond vector search itself. An in-process engine plus VSS says something important about system shape: sometimes a simpler local architecture teaches more than adding distributed infrastructure too early.

embedded analytics
fast iteration
less infrastructure gravity

EVA

Event-driven agent systems as an alternative to direct agent-to-agent coupling. The lesson is to choose the communication style that keeps the system easier to evolve, observe, and change over time.

event-driven
loose coupling
observability

First, a pattern appears in isolated work. Then it shows up in slightly more people's frustrations. Then vendors package pieces of it. Then the language stabilizes. Then the thing that once felt "too early" becomes a category. That is what an architect learns to recognize — before everyone else does.

07

Insights from experience

These are the kind of sentences that become true after enough systems push back.

“Patterns are scaffolding for thinking, not commandments. The moment you treat them as commandments, you stop being an architect and become a parrot.”
“Most teams adopt CQRS for the wrong reasons. It earns its complexity in maybe one system in ten. Knowing the other nine is the architectural skill.”
“A clean diagram and a painful codebase are not a contradiction. They are the most common outcome.”
“Distributed systems are mostly about coordination costs. Every network boundary buys you time, failure, and uncertainty whether you wanted them or not.”
“Modernization is not a project. It is a posture you adopt for the rest of your career.”
“The job is rarely choosing the most advanced option. The job is choosing the option that keeps the next change possible.”

Closing the session

End with a reusable set of questions students can apply to any new tool, trend, or pattern.

Four useful questions

  • What problem is this approach trying to solve?
  • What trade-off does it introduce?
  • What kind of team, domain, or scale makes it a good fit?
  • What kind of future change does it make easier or harder?
